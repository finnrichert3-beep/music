          Audition day

          • Eat, and get there 30 minutes early. Warm your hands under warm water if the room is cold.
          • Warm up with slow scales in the keys of both pieces. Do not run either piece at full speed.
          • Before each piece, take one breath and set the tempo from the opening measure in your head.
          • Sight-reading: do your 30-second scan in your usual order, set the tempo from the hardest measure, and do not stop. Keep the rhythm going even when notes go wrong.
          • If something slips, jump to the next rehearsal letter and keep going. You have practiced this.

          How this plan worksEach week has a date range and a target. Three checkpoints tell you if you are on track.

          Nine weeks is tight, so everything runs at once

          The earlier version of this plan assumed four to eight months with one phase after another. You have 63 days. So flashcards, sight-reading, and work on the pieces all start this week and run side by side. What changes from week to week is how much time each one gets.

          Be realistic about sight-reading. In nine weeks, rung 3 played reliably is a good result, and rung 4 is a stretch goal. Panels care more that you keep going without stopping than that you play hard music, and that is something you can practice.

          What it takes each day

          45 to 55 minutes, six days a week. The schedule is laid out under “The daily session” on the Today tab. On busy days, do the 10-minute flashcard block and two sight-reading excerpts, and skip the rest. That short version still keeps your streak going.

          Each checkpoint has a “behind?” line. If you have not cleared a checkpoint by its date, follow that line and cut what it says to cut. Do not push the whole schedule later, because December 7 will not move.

          Sight-reading rules

          • Never stop and never go back. A wrong note in time is fine. A pause is a failure.
          • Set the tempo from the hardest measure. It will feel very slow, and that is correct.
          • Scan for 30 seconds first, in the same order every time.
          • Keep your eyes on the page. If you have to look down, the excerpt is too hard.
          • Read each excerpt once. A second read is practice, not sight-reading.
          • Rhythm matters more than pitch. Drop notes before you drop the beat.
          • Move up a rung after three clean days. Move down after two days with breakdowns.

          When you stall

          • You name notes, but slowly. You are still counting up from a landmark. Drill five notes at a time until each one is a picture.
          • You name them fine but play them slowly. You are going through the letter name. Skip naming and do only note-to-key for a week.
          • Sight-reading keeps falling apart. The music is too hard or too fast. Drop a rung and halve the tempo.
          • Your eyes keep dropping to your hands. Cover the keys with a cloth while you read.
          • Muscle memory takes over from the score. Play at quarter tempo, in an odd rhythm, or one hand at a time.
          • You have no motivation. Do the 10-minute flashcard block and nothing else. A short session is far better than a missed one.

          About the sax years. Your treble reading was trained on a single line in a transposing key. The piano asks for two staves, stacked notes, and concert pitch, so treble may feel slower for the first couple of weeks. It will catch up.

          Learn it from the score, not a visualizer. You already know Van Gogh and Woke Up in Japan by ear — this one's different: build it straight from notation from day one. It doubles as reading practice on top of being an audition piece, and you won't have muscle memory fighting the page later.

          Why it fits

          • Impressionistic era, clearly contrasting the Passacaglia's Baroque drive — sustained, colorful, free, where the Passacaglia is strict and driving.
          • Standalone piece. Though grouped with Gymnopédies No. 2–3, No. 1 alone is a complete, independent work — fine to play on its own.
          • Left hand is a repeating octave/chord pattern; right hand is a simple, singable melodic line — a good first piece to build real notation-reading on.
          • Easy arrangements are widely available, full-length (not excerpt-only), so there's no risk of landing a "theme only" version like some Clair de Lune arrangements have.
